Benchmark and method

Why area × delay is the score

Every design is scored by its area-delay product (ADP): the cell area of the synthesized netlist in µm² times its critical-path delay in ps. The critical path bounds the clock period, and the testbench fixes how many cycles the job takes, so a design's throughput is proportional to 1 / delay. Its throughput per unit silicon is therefore proportional to 1 / ADP: halving the ADP means the same die area delivers twice the compute, whether you get there by a smaller circuit, a faster one, or both. That is what makes ADP a meaningful single number rather than an arbitrary blend of two metrics.

It also keeps the optimization honest. Neither axis can be bought with the other at face value: a design that shrinks area by 20 % but lengthens the critical path by 25 % scores worse, and one that speeds up by duplicating hardware only wins if the speed-up exceeds the extra area. Because the cycle count is pinned by the cycle-accurate testbench, the agent cannot trade latency for area either; it has to find genuinely better logic.
